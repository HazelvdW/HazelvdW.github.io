Research

I'm a researcher in music psychology with a deep fascination for imagination — specifically, the extraordinary variety of inner worlds and experiences that music colours in each of us. From vivid visual scenes to half-formed feelings to memories you'd forgotten you had, I find endless wonder in the fact that the same piece of music can open up something completely unique in every listener.

I'm also a passionate advocate for Open Research: transparent methods, shared data, and science that anyone can scrutinise, build on, or just stumble into with curiosity.

Thesis, PhD Project

Completed! · PhD

Music & Imagination: An Empirical Mixed-Method Venture into Music-Influenced Mental Content.

Durham University · Music Department · 2024–2026

My PhD explored the interaction of music with imagination —music-influenced mental content (MIMC)— the images, stories, memories, and abstract experiences that arise during music listening, Rather than treating MIMC as a natural kind with fixed properties, I approached it as a relational event: something that comes into being at the intersection of music, listener, and context, and that cannot be fully understood by studying any one of these in isolation. Starting from within the pervasive empirical cognitivist framework characteristing music & imagination studies, coming to a constructive relational realist standpoint of imagination through the act of *doing* methodologically pluralistic research.

Four empirical papers build this account from the ground up: (1) curation of a music stimulus set with accompanying normative data; (2) systematic mapping of thought-types experienced across varied music stimuli; (3) computational analysis of how extramusical context information shapes thought contents; (4) a large-scale mixed-method qualitative study producing a comprehensive typology of music-influenced mental content. Together, these chapters weave a layered empirical account of the rich inner thoughtscapes that music, the listener, and their learned & shared frames of the world can co-create in a relational event. Using a blend of quantitative and qualitative methods captures both the statistical patterns and the irreducibly human variety that only qualitative depth can reach within our nuanced musically sculpted imaginations.

Research Interests

Open & Reproducible Research

Good research should be visible, sharable, and challengeable. I care deeply about open data, transparent methods, and building a culture where science is something we do together — not something we hoard.

Human Nuance in Data

Numbers can tell you a lot, but they miss the texture. I'm drawn to mixed methods — the messiness of qualitative work alongside the order of quantitative analysis — because reality tends to live somewhere in between.

Imagination & the Listening Mind

Music can send the mind somewhere entirely else — and I'm captivated by where it goes. Images, stories, memories, abstract experiences that bloom amongst the notes; and the fact that this unfolds so differently for each person makes it a uniquely revealing window into imagination itself.

Context, Genre & Meaning-Making

A title, a backstory, a listening setting — small nudges that can reshape an entire experience. Alongside this, I believe genre is never just a footnote: from basslines to beehive haircuts, research should reflect the genuine diversity of how people actually listen.

Basslines, Bodies & Beehive Haircuts: What do we think about when we listen to music?

van der Walle, H. A., Margulis, E. H., & Jakubowski, K. · Imagination, Cognition and Personality

A large-scale mixed-method qualitative study of 14,091 music-influenced mental content (MIMC) descriptions across 10 genres. Preprint · 2024 MSc Paper

Experiencing liveness from recorded music

Rai, L., van der Walle, H. A., Painting, J., & Orgs, G.

An investigation into what extent listeners perceive and experience a sense of social liveness — the perceived presence of other people — from recorded music by adding audience reactions to recordings, and what this reveals about the aesthetic experience of recorded live performance. These findings show how social liveness can mimic actual live events, showcasing the importance of social influences for cultural engagement.
